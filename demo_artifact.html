This village runs whether or not you play it. Households gather, trade, hoard, and freeze on their own. But they are myopic — they see only about four days ahead, so every year they walk into winter unprepared and every year they suffer for it.

That myopia makes the village a clean instrument for a single question: can information itself — not force, not money, just a claim that travels — change what happens? To find out, we don't touch the economy. We whisper one sentence to one person and watch it move.

Finding I — Information as contribution

A true warning, given away, averts the crisis

Before the first winter, one villager is told the truth: cold is coming, and there won't be enough wood. That claim spreads through the village's social graph — neighbour to neighbour, through the tavern that everyone passes. Households that hear it, and believe it, begin to stockpile ahead of a winter they could not otherwise foresee. Below: the wood each village had banked as winter (shaded) arrived.

The warned village (ember) climbs to a deep reserve before the cold; the control (steel) drifts along, then crashes as winter drains it. The warning cost nothing and produced nothing — it only pointed foresight at a village that had none. In the economy's own ledger, that act scores as contribution worth +29.5 — the same realized-effect accounting the simulation uses for goods, now applied to a sentence.

Finding II — Popularity is not truth

The same machinery carries a lie — but not as far

Spreading works both ways, so we test a false claim too: a rumour of famine, whispered to one villager in a calm autumn when nothing is wrong. It travels on exactly the same network. The question isn't whether it spreads — it does — but whether being widely heard is the same as being widely believed.

Both claims reach most of the village. But the rumour traces back to one mouth repeating itself — an echo — and the model caps how far a single source can raise belief. The real shortage is witnessed independently by nine households going hungry at once; those separate roots corroborate, and belief climbs to conviction. The lie is known and stays merely plausible; the truth is confirmed and drives action. Being repeated is not the same as being real.

A result we didn't get — and won't hide

We tried hard to make a lie cause a real famine — the classic “panic becomes self-fulfilling.” In this economy it refuses to. Wood and food can always be gathered, so a scare just makes households over-provision, and the panic ends up protective, not fatal.

That's a genuine finding, not a bug: for misinformation to manufacture real scarcity you need supply that can't stretch, or a group that depends on buying rather than making. That inelasticity is the next ingredient to build — and the point at which a lie-for-profit becomes the mirror image of hoarding.

Why it matters

The village already paid people for goods that did real work. Now it can pay them for information that does — and charge those whose words do harm.

A true signal that stabilises the world is contribution. A false one spread for gain is hoarding in another medium. Both are the same axis, measured the same way — by what actually happened downstream. That's the moment “knowledge is value” stops being a slogan and becomes a number the world can settle. This demo is the first working slice of it.
