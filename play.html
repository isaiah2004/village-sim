What just decided all of that

Spread

A belief is retold only when it's notable enough (pressure = how-much × how-sure, over a threshold), and only to people you're connected to. The tavern reaches everyone; a whisper reaches one. Each retelling loses fidelity, so belief fades with distance.

Conviction

Hearing something raises your certainty by how much you trust the source × how new it is to you. Hearing the same original source again barely moves you — that's an echo. This is why a loud liar still can't make you sure.

A race between panic and proof

“Wood's gone now” is checkable — people can look at the woodshed. Given time, and a source they doubt, they verify it and stop passing it on. But a fast, credible panic makes them hoard before anyone checks — and once the shelves really are bare, the lie has made itself true. Weak or slow ⇒ debunked; loud, trusted, and urgent ⇒ it wins the race.

Worth (the score)

Separate from spread: your words are scored by what actually changed. Belief that made the village prepare for a real winter = contribution. Belief that just made noise = nothing. Same accounting the sim uses to reward real work — now applied to information.

The bigger picture

What this little game is a slice of

This is one mechanic pulled out of a much larger project: a fantasy world that runs as a living simulation, where a player enters an already-functioning civilization and reshapes it through real systemic impact — not scripted quests. Think of a modern mind dropped into a medieval world: your power isn't a sword, it's knowing how economies, knowledge, and institutions actually work, and using that edge to change the world's course.

The core idea — you're paid for contribution, not grinding

Most games reward time spent: kill 10 boars, repeat. This world has an AI Accountant that watches the whole economy and rewards you for realized effect — did your action actually make the world better off? Stabilise a market, prevent a shortage, get supplies to where they were needed, and you're paid for the good that actually happened. Corner a resource and let people suffer, and the same system charges you for it. Hoarding is structurally worthless; contribution is the currency. This little demo is that exact logic applied to information instead of goods.

Three systems, one idea

01
A living economy

Villagers produce, trade, and consume on their own; markets find prices; winter bites. It runs with or without you — you're an agent inside it, not its protagonist. This is the validated core the accountant sits on top of.

Built & validated
02
Minds that only know their world

No NPC is omniscient. A fisherman knows local rumours; a scholar knows history; a king knows geopolitics. Knowledge is gated by role, place, and standing — and can be lost if its source is gone. Believable intelligence through controlled ignorance.

In design
03
Knowledge that travels

Information isn't a fact everyone shares — it spreads person to person, with delay, distortion, and echoes, and it changes what people do. That's the layer you just played. Beliefs move the world, whether they're true or not.

Prototyped — this demo

The throughline that ties it together

Goods and information turn out to be the same axis. A true, timely warning that helps the village prepare is a contribution — exactly like handing over wood that keeps someone warm. A false panic spread for advantage is hoarding in another medium — it corners the vulnerable and destroys value. The accountant can score both the same way: by what actually happened downstream.

It's the moment “knowledge is value” stops being a slogan and becomes a number the world can settle.

How it's being built, and where it's going

The approach is bottom-up: prove each system as a small, honest, headless model first, then carry the validated version into a full 3D world (Unreal). It's meant to work as both a real game and a research testbed for how agents make good systemic decisions.

DoneThe contribution-indexed economy — built and validated against clean control scenarios.

NextKnowledge-gated NPC dialogue, and a “world-change” engine: an authored map of cause and effect that an AI can propose additions to, with the simulation itself acting as the fact-checker before anything becomes real.

One honest note, visible in the game above: for a lie to truly bite, the world needs people who depend on others — a group that can't just make more for themselves. Give a village that vulnerability and misinformation becomes genuinely dangerous. Take it away and panic just becomes harmless over-provisioning. That dependency is what makes information a matter of life and death — and what the deeper economy is being built to model.
